Online sleep-apnea services promise a prescription without an office visit. Some are legitimate telehealth; others sell a questionnaire. Here's how to tell the difference, using FDA rules, AASM guidance and Medicare policy.

## Why you need a prescription

ResMed's AirSense 11 user guide states: "In the US, Federal law restricts this device to sale by or on the order of a physician" ([AirSense 11 guide](https://document.resmed.com/documents/products/machine/airsense-11/user-guide/airsense11_user-guide_amer_mul.pdf)). That's the FDA prescription-device framework ([21 CFR 801.109](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-21/chapter-I/subchapter-H/part-801/subpart-D/section-801.109)): devices that aren't safe without practitioner supervision are sold only on a licensed practitioner's order. So a legitimate U.S. seller should ask for your prescription before shipping a machine.

## What a legitimate online pathway looks like

| Step | Legitimate service | Red flag |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Evaluation | A licensed clinician reviews your history and symptoms | Only an online quiz |
| Diagnosis | A sleep test: home sleep apnea test or in-lab study | "Prescription" issued from a questionnaire alone |
| Result review | Clinician interprets the test and discusses severity and options | No report shared with you |
| Prescription | Specifies device type and pressure settings | Vague or missing settings |
| Follow-up | Check-in on comfort, leaks and data | No follow-up offered |

The AASM strongly recommends that "clinical tools, questionnaires and prediction algorithms not be used to diagnose OSA in adults, in the absence of polysomnography or home sleep apnea testing" ([AASM 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5337595/)). Home testing is appropriate for uncomplicated adults; people with significant heart or lung disease, neuromuscular conditions, opioid use, stroke or severe insomnia should have an in-lab study. See [home vs in-lab sleep tests](/us/guides/home-sleep-test-vs-in-lab/).

## If you're on Medicare

Medicare's PAP policy ([L33718](https://www.cms.gov/medicare-coverage-database/view/lcd.aspx?lcdid=33718)) requires:

- an **in-person** clinical evaluation by the treating practitioner before the sleep test
- a qualifying Medicare-valid sleep test
- instruction from the supplier on using the device
- a 12-week trial with adherence of at least 4 hours a night on 70% of nights in a 30-day period

The policy text uses "in-person". HHZ couldn't verify from the LCD whether a telehealth visit satisfies it, so ask your practitioner and DME supplier before paying for an online service. Details: [Medicare CPAP coverage](/us/guides/medicare-cpap-coverage-compliance/).

## Checklist before you pay

| Ask | Why |
| --- | --- |
| Who's the clinician, and are they licensed in my state? | You need a legitimate prescriber |
| What sleep test is used? Is it FDA-cleared? | Questionnaires alone aren't diagnostic (AASM) |
| Will I get the full test report? | Useful for any future supplier or payer |
| What's in the prescription (device type, pressures, mask)? | Sellers need it |
| What if the test is negative or inconclusive? | AASM: in-lab study should follow |
| Is there follow-up after I start? | Early problems such as leaks need adjusting ([mask leak guide](/us/guides/cpap-mask-leak/)) |
| Will my insurer accept it? | Payer rules vary; Medicare says in-person evaluation |

## Verdict

An online CPAP prescription is legitimate when it's based on a real sleep test interpreted by a licensed clinician, not on a quiz. For cash buyers it can be a fast, convenient route. If you're on Medicare or need insurance to pay, confirm first that the evaluation format meets your plan's rules.
