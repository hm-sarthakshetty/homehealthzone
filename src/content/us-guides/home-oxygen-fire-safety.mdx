Medical oxygen isn't flammable itself, but it changes how fires behave. The NFPA puts it simply: "Fire needs oxygen to burn. If a fire should start in an oxygen-enriched area, the material burning will burn more quickly" ([NFPA](https://www.nfpa.org/downloadable-resources/safety-tip-sheets/medical-oxygen-safety-tip-sheet)). The VA adds that oxygen "acts as an accelerant, which causes nearby material to ignite more easily and burn more intensely", and that concentrators, cylinders and liquid oxygen all carry this risk ([VA Patient Safety Alert AL18-01](https://www.patientsafety.va.gov/docs/alerts/INTERNET_AL18-01.pdf)).

## How common are home oxygen fires?

Between October 2006 and October 2016 the VA's National Center for Patient Safety received 746 reports of burns or fires involving home oxygen. It has had over 70 reports a year since 2010. Smoking is the leading ignition source. The other reported sources are listed below in the VA's order, from most to least reported ([VA](https://www.patientsafety.va.gov/docs/alerts/INTERNET_AL18-01.pdf)):

| Rank | Reported ignition source |
| --- | --- |
| 1 | Smoking |
| 2 | Open flames (candles, gas stoves, incense) |
| 3 | Power tools |
| 4 | Power strips |
| 5 | Static electricity |
| 6 | Portable electronic devices |
| 7 | Grills/barbecues |
| 8 | Welding |
| 9 | Electric heaters |
| 10 | CPAP devices |
| 11 | Pilot lights |
| 12 | Firearms |

## Smoking: no safe way

Massachusetts' fire services: "There is no safe way to smoke around home oxygen. Turning off the oxygen is not enough because your clothes, hair, bedding and the tubing are oxygen-enriched." Until someone can quit, they advise: disconnect the oxygen, wait ten minutes, and go outside. They add that this "is not a safe way to smoke" but lowers the danger ([Mass.gov](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/home-oxygen-safety)). Inogen's Rove 6 manual likewise says smoking during oxygen therapy "is likely to result in facial burns or death" ([Rove 6 manual](https://www.inogen.com/rove6-user-manuals/pdf/96-13595-00-01%20A%20Inogen%20Rove%206%20US%20User%20Manual_EN_ES_February%202026.pdf)). MedlinePlus says no one should smoke in a room where oxygen is used, and suggests a NO SMOKING sign in every such room ([MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000049.htm)).

## Distance from heat and flame

| Source | Distance | Applies to |
| --- | --- | --- |
| [MedlinePlus](https://medlineplus.gov/ency/patientinstructions/000049.htm) | 6 feet (about 2 m) | Electric-motor toys, baseboard or space heaters, wood stoves, fireplaces, candles, electric blankets, hairdryers, electric razors, electric toothbrushes; also stoves and tabletop candles in restaurants |
| [Mass.gov](https://www.mass.gov/info-details/home-oxygen-safety) | 10 feet | Oxygen and tubing from candles, matches, lighters, heaters, wood stoves, electric razors, hair dryers, cooking stoves and smoking materials |
| [Inogen Rove 6 manual](https://www.inogen.com/rove6-user-manuals/pdf/96-13595-00-01%20A%20Inogen%20Rove%206%20US%20User%20Manual_EN_ES_February%202026.pdf) | 2 m (6.56 ft) | Smoking or open flames near the device while in use |

Check your own device manual. When sources differ, the larger distance gives the bigger margin.

## Home checklist

| Area | What to do | Source |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Alarms | Working smoke alarms; test monthly | MedlinePlus, Mass.gov |
| Extinguisher | Keep a working one; more than one if you move around the house with oxygen | MedlinePlus |
| Escape plan | 2 ways out of every room plus an outdoor meeting place; practice twice a year | Mass.gov |
| Kitchen | Keep oxygen away from the stovetop and oven; watch for grease splatter; a microwave is usually OK | MedlinePlus |
| Storage | Not in a trunk, box or small closet; under the bed is OK if air moves freely | MedlinePlus |
| Flammable liquids | Keep oil-, grease- or alcohol-based cleaners away from oxygen | MedlinePlus |
| Skin and lips | No petroleum-based creams or oil-based lip balms unless your provider approves; aloe vera and water-based products are safe | MedlinePlus, Mass.gov |
| Device | Don't oil or grease fittings; avoid aerosol sprays near the device; use only the maker's power supplies | Rove 6 manual |
| Tubing | Avoid trip hazards; tape tubing to the back of your shirt; teach children not to get tangled | MedlinePlus |

## Thermal fuses (firebreaks)

A thermal fuse sits in line with the tubing. It "melts when heated and blocks the flow of oxygen" in a fire, removing a major accelerant. The VA requires them in its home oxygen contracts. But the VA stresses the limits: a fuse "will not prevent burns to the face, neck, and airways if the ignition occurs at or near the nasal cannula", doesn't start or stop a fire, and "does not make it safe to smoke while using oxygen" ([VA](https://www.patientsafety.va.gov/docs/alerts/INTERNET_AL18-01.pdf)). Ask your supplier whether your tubing has one.

## With CPAP and other equipment

CPAP devices appear on the VA's list of reported ignition sources. If you bleed oxygen into a CPAP, follow your clinician's setup and the CPAP manual's oxygen warnings. See [CPAP with oxygen](/us/guides/cpap-with-oxygen/).

## Verdict

The rules come down to five habits: no smoking, distance from heat and flame (at least 6 feet, 10 for more margin), no petroleum products, ventilated storage, and working smoke alarms with an escape plan. Add a thermal fuse, but don't treat it as permission to smoke. The ATS guideline recommends patient and caregiver education on safety ([ATS](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7667898/)), so ask for a home safety check.
